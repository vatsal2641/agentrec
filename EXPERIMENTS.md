# EXPERIMENTS.md

> **Every number on this page comes from the SYNTHETIC dataset** (MovieLens-1M file format, generated
> by `scripts/make_synthetic_data.py`). The build machine had no internet access. Bandit and feedback
> numbers also come from a **user simulator**. To get MovieLens numbers, run
> `bash scripts/run_all.sh configs/ml1m.yaml` and read `results/ml1m/ALL_RESULTS.md`.
>
> **Reproducibility check:** a fresh `git clone` followed by `bash scripts/run_all.sh configs/synthetic.yaml`
> reproduced every metric below exactly; only the timing columns differ. This page is generated by
> `scripts/build_experiments_md.py`: every number in the tables and in the prose is read from
> `results/synthetic/`, and none was typed by hand.

Protocol for all offline experiments:
- Global temporal split (80/90 timestamp percentiles).
- Models are tuned on VAL and refit on TRAIN+VAL before scoring TEST.
- Full-catalogue ranking, with seen items masked.
- A rating ≥ 4 counts as a positive.
- Seed 42.

**Reading it**
- The best learned model is **itemknn** at NDCG@10 = 0.2141. That is
  3.8× popularity (0.0564) and
  3.1× recent popularity (0.0681).
- Item-kNN, BPR-MF and the two-tower land within a few percent of each other on NDCG. The two-tower
  has the best Recall@50 (0.3719).
- Long-tail recall@10 is highest for **content** (0.0363). Among the collaborative
  models, the two-tower is best (0.0278, against item-kNN 0.0143 and BPR
  0.0157). It's plausible that its genre features help, but no ablation here isolates that.
- The content model has the highest coverage but low accuracy. That's the classic
  over-specialisation and quality-blindness trade-off.
- Cold users (no positives in the fit period) get a popularity fallback. For every model it is
  all-time popularity (0.0907),
  except recent popularity, whose fallback is its own recency list
  (0.0911).
- Caveat: the synthetic generator is a latent-factor model, which favours factorisation models.
  Don't generalise these gaps to MovieLens.

**Reading it**
- Candidate recall rises steeply with N. This is the ceiling for the whole system: the ranker can't
  recover what retrieval drops.
- The 10% popularity slice doesn't help warm users here (the union is slightly below embedding-only).
  It is there for cold and sparse users, where popularity is the only signal.
- IVF recall against exact search rises with n_probe as expected. But **our numpy IVF is not faster**
  than exact search at 2000 items. The Python loop overhead is bigger than the saved dot
  products. ANN pays off at scale: brute force was measured at
  9.2 ns per item, which extrapolates to
  about 92 ms per query at 10M
  items. That figure is an extrapolation, not a measurement.

Pointwise LR coefficients (standardised features): {'retrieval_score': 0.4564, 'retrieval_rank': -0.434, 'itemknn_score': 0.1913, 'log_pop': -0.1938, 'log_recent_pop': 0.4068, 'genre_match': 0.1535, 'item_age': -0.0542, 'log_user_activity': -0.0467, 'from_popular_source': 0.0043}

**Reading it**
- The ranker chosen on held-out VAL users is **gbdt**. On TEST it scores NDCG@10 = 0.2395,
  against 0.2226 for retrieval order: **+7.6%**.
  This is the number to quote.
- Every learned ranker beats retrieval order on TEST. The best on TEST is pointwise
  (0.2426), which is *not* the one chosen on VAL. Quoting it would be
  cherry-picking. Without repeated splits we can't say how much of the gap between rankers is noise.
- **More candidates made NDCG slightly worse.** There are two candidate explanations, and this run
  can't tell them apart:
  - The weak ranker is hurt by extra distractors.
  - The ranker was trained with N = 200, so the `retrieval_rank` feature (position / N) means
    something different at other N (a train/serve feature mismatch).

  Either way, choose N by measurement, not by "bigger is better".
- **No retrieval (rank the whole catalogue):** NDCG@10 = 0.1922 against
  0.2168 with retrieval on the same users, and it is slower.
  The ranker was trained on retrieved candidates, so on the full catalogue it faces a distribution it
  never saw. Retrieval is a filter as well as a speed-up.
- The candidate positive rate is 0.037, so ranker training is very
  imbalanced.

## E5 new users, 15 sessions each (200 users, history hidden; 2 seeds)

Arms: `no_adaptation` = ranker top-K, state never updated; `user_state_update` = ranker top-K + user-state updates; `user_state_and_policy_update` = **LinUCB slate selection** + user-state + policy updates (two changes vs the previous arm).

| condition | exp. clicks session 1 | session last | mean over sessions |
|---|---|---|---|
| no_adaptation | 0.1740 | 0.1740 | 0.1740 |
| user_state_update | 0.1740 | 0.3414 | 0.3162 |
| user_state_and_policy_update | 0.1303 | 0.3730 | 0.3153 |

**Reading it**
- **E4a (toy bandit):** Thompson sampling has the lowest regret. **UCB1 is worse than ε-greedy at
  T = 5,000**, even though UCB1 has the better asymptotic guarantee. Its bonus
  (√(2 ln t / n)) is conservative at finite horizons.
- **E4:** every learning policy beats the frozen offline ranker. Greedy online learning cuts regret by
  35% and default LinUCB by 34%.
  **No exploration policy reliably beats greedy:**
  - Defaults: LinUCB 4740, LinTS 5061, ε-greedy 5136, against
    greedy 4673 ± 189 (sd over seeds).
  - The best sweep settings, which were picked after the fact on the same simulator, are LinUCB
    α = 0.5 (4653) and LinTS v = 0.1
    (4615). Both are within greedy's seed spread.

  One likely reason: 500 diverse users provide a lot of "free" exploration through context variety.
  See Bastani, Bayati & Khosravi, *Mostly Exploration-Free Algorithms for Contextual Bandits*,
  Management Science 2021.
- **The warm start doesn't hold for UCB and TS at t = 0.** The prior mean reproduces the ranker's
  order, but LinUCB's bonus α‖x‖/√λ and LinTS's sampling noise are much larger than the small prior
  term. So their first slates are mostly exploration, and only greedy and ε-greedy start at the
  ranker. A larger λ shrinks the bonus by 1/√λ, which **mitigates but does not remove** this. An
  independent check at t = 0 over 100 users found LinUCB's first slate matched the ranker's top 5 for
  9 users at λ = 1 and 30 users at λ = 100.
- **E4b (position bias):** learning with position as a feature beats naive updates. Regret is
  5570 naive, against
  4740 with the position
  feature, and 4497 for the
  oracle that knows which items were examined. The fix closes most of the gap.
- **E6 (noise), 2 seeds:**
  - LinUCB with the stronger prior (λ = 100) has the lowest mean regret at every noise level. At
    p = 0 it scores 4370, against
    4753 for λ = 1. At p = 0.1 the gap is inside the seed spread.
  - At p = 0.3, greedy's regret becomes very unstable (7017 ± 1566)
    and averages close to the frozen ranker (7166). LinUCB with λ = 1 gets
    5834, and with λ = 100 5433.
  - So both exploration and prior strength matter under heavy noise. With 2 seeds, don't over-read
    the ordering.
- **E5 (new users, full feedback loop):**
  - Updating the user state from clicks, skips and ratings takes mean expected clicks per slate from
    0.174 (no adaptation) to 0.316.
  - The third arm changes **two** things: LinUCB chooses the slate instead of the ranker's top K, and
    the policy is updated online. It reaches the same mean (0.315).
    It costs clicks in session 1 (0.130 vs
    0.174, which fits the un-warm-started bonus described above) and ends
    higher (0.373 vs 0.341).
  - We can't attribute that difference to the policy update alone.
- **Assumptions behind all of E4–E6:**
  - The truth model is the generator's latent factors.
  - Examination follows a PBM with e_k = 1/k^0.7.
  - Clicks are immediate, and preferences are stationary.
  - Slates are K independent picks.

## E8: Request mode (agent) vs fixed pipeline

Columns: *valid & unseen* = every returned id exists in the catalogue and was not already seen by the user (membership in the retrieved set is enforced separately by the `recommend` tool). Rates are per returned item, except *filled k* (per turn).

**Reading it**
- **feed** ignores the request, so it misses soft constraints (decade, hidden gems).
- **fixed** (one filtered retrieval, no replanning, no memory):
  - It fills only 0.73 of tight decade requests,
    because it returns fewer than k items when too few match.
  - On the memory follow-up it doesn't know the stated dislike. The measured effect is small, because
    a random disliked genre rarely lands in the top 5: 0.013
    of items violate it, against 0.000
    for the agent.
- **agent_rule** fills every slate, with zero hard-constraint violations. It replanned (relaxed
  constraints) on 40 requests. The price of relaxation is
  lower soft-constraint satisfaction on decade requests
  (0.74 vs 0.89).
  That's a deliberate trade-off, and the explanations tell the user about it.
- **agent_halluc**: the test harness injects one out-of-catalogue item id into the first `recommend`
  call of every turn (1200 turns). The `recommend` tool rejected all
  1200, and every final output consisted of valid,
  retrieved ids. This is a harness-injected stress test, not a measurement of how often an LLM
  hallucinates.
- **What this does NOT show:** whether an LLM planner beats the rule-based one on messy language.
  The template requests are exactly the kind of input a regex can parse. Run `--llm` with your own
  messy requests to test that. It's the key open question for the "agentic" claim.
